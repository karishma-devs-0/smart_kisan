SmartKisan · Model Accuracy Report · 11 September 2026

How accurate are the two AI features, really?

Both models were previously described by their training accuracy, which is measured on photographs from the same collection they learned from. This report measures them instead against photographs taken somewhere else, in real conditions — which is the only kind the app ever receives.

Prepared for Hbeonlabs Technologies Disease test set 236 field photographs Weed test sets 1,742 images

Why the earlier figures were not wrong, but were not useful

The disease model was reported at 99% accurate. That figure is real, and it is measured on PlantVillage: a single leaf, detached from the plant, laid on a plain background under even studio light.

A farmer photographs a leaf still attached to the plant, in sunlight, with soil and other foliage behind it. Against that kind of photograph the same model scores 28%. Nothing changed about the model between those two numbers — only the photographs.

Every figure below is measured against PlantDoc for disease and four separate unseen collections for weeds. None of these images were used in training, and none share a camera or a field with the training data.

The two shaded rows matter more than the headline. The live model answers “diseased” to almost anything — its 28% is not spread evenly, it has its thumb on the scale. Two out of every five healthy plants photographed come back with a fungicide recommendation attached.

The retrained model corrects that, and the honest cost is the fourth row: missed infections rise from 3% to 9%. We judged that the better trade. A missed detection leaves a farmer where they started; a false alarm takes money for a chemical and puts it on a plant that was fine.

When the app should decline to answer

58% correct is not enough to state a diagnosis every time. The app withholds an answer below a confidence threshold and asks for a clearer photograph instead. We measured where that threshold belongs rather than guessing.

Weed detection

This model runs on the phone rather than a server, and identifies whether a plant is the crop, a grass weed or a broadleaf weed — the distinction that decides which herbicide to buy. It is unchanged this cycle; what changed is that we can now say how well it works.

The weakness that matters most

On the 684 unseen drone images, only 13% of grass weeds were correctly called grass — 203 of 234 read as broadleaf instead. Grass versus broadleaf is exactly the distinction that decides which herbicide a farmer buys, so this matters more than the headline accuracy. It is the priority for the next round of work.

The app already flags any result below 60% confidence with a prompt to look closer rather than act. The note shown to the farmer was also corrected this cycle — it had been describing a model replaced in August.

Four things we tried that did not work

Recorded because each cost real time, and because the results are the reason the current settings are where they are. Every one of them looked better on the training-style measurement and worse on real photographs.

Adding a second weed dataset (DeepWeeds)

87% → 53%

Contributes broadleaf examples only, and broadleaf was already at 100% recall. It strengthened the class that needed no help while its dry Australian backgrounds gave the model something easier to learn than leaf shape. Grass recall collapsed from 75% to 25%.

Adding drone imagery of a cotton field (CoFly)

87% → 73%

Shot straight down from five metres. No handheld photograph resembles that viewpoint, so it dilutes the signal that matters without adding a useful one.

A larger, longer disease training run

58% → 55%

More of the network unfrozen, nearly twice the epochs. Scored higher on validation — 78.5% against 77.3% — and worse on held-out field photographs, with missed infections rising from 8% to 10%.

Weighting rare diseases more heavily

58% → 56%

Intended to lift the weakest classes, which were also the scarcest. Those were precisely the ones that did not move: mosaic virus stayed at 10% recall and leaf mold at 17%. They are not merely rare, they are hard — weighting cannot supply variety that is not in the data.

The common thread is worth stating plainly: more images do not help when they were captured differently from how the app is used. Test-time averaging was also measured — it moves both error rates about two points the right way for 3.4× the inference cost, which is not worth several extra seconds per scan on a free hosting tier.

These are not Indian field conditions. PlantDoc is the closest public collection of real field photographs available, but it is not photographs of Indian farms taken on the phones our users carry. The true figure for our farmers is unmeasured, and could be either side of these numbers.

The weed model judges “is this my crop” against sorghum seedlings, because sorghum is the only collection with labelled crop close-ups. A farmer photographing wheat is being compared to the wrong plant.

Both models are now limited by photographs, not by code. Roughly 50 real photographs per category — wheat, rice and cotton for weeds; mosaic virus and leaf mold for disease — would be worth more than any further tuning we can do.
