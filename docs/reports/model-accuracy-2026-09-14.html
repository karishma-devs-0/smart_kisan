SmartKisan · Model Accuracy Report · 14 September 2026

Rebuilt around the crops Indian farmers actually grow

The disease model has gone from 28% to 86.9% correct on real field photographs. Most of that came not from better training, but from discovering the model was answering the wrong question: a third of its classes were apple, grape and strawberry, and it had never seen wheat or rice at all.

Prepared for Hbeonlabs Technologies Held-out test set 1,581 photographs Training set 20,777, of which 78% field

The problem was the question, not the answer

The deployed model classifies 38 diseases. Sixteen of them are apple, grape, cherry, peach, blueberry, raspberry and strawberry — temperate orchard and berry crops that a smallholder in Punjab or Tamil Nadu does not grow.

Worse, wheat and rice had no classes at all. A farmer photographing his wheat received a confident answer about a tomato, because tomato was the nearest thing the model knew. No amount of accuracy would have fixed that.

The label space was replaced: orchard and berry crops out, rice and wheat in. 32 classes across 9 crops.

Read this before quoting 88% for tomato

Tomato, potato and maize are now measured largely on the new collections, whose train/test splits were made by us rather than published by the dataset authors. Those are the optimistic end.

The PlantDoc portion — the only split published by someone else, and therefore the hardest test — is 57%, unchanged. The honest reading: the model is much better on photographs resembling real farm photography, and the toughest benchmark did not move.

Four attempts failed. Real photographs worked.

Every training-side technique was tried on the weak crops first. All of them used PlantVillage, which is laboratory imagery — one detached leaf on a plain background. The test is field imagery. More laboratory data does not teach a model to separate two blights on a phone camera in a field.

A larger, longer training run

58% → 55%

More of the network unfrozen, nearly twice the epochs. Scored higher on validation and worse on held-out field photographs, with missed infections rising.

Averaging over multiple views of each photo

no gain

Accuracy unchanged for 3.4× the inference cost — several extra seconds on every scan.

Weighting rare diseases more heavily

58% → 56%

Intended to lift the scarcest classes. Those were precisely the ones that did not move. They are not merely rare, they are hard.

Rebalancing: more lab images for the weak crops

44% → 38%

Tomato was raised from 120 to 400 laboratory images per disease and rice cut back. Tomato got worse; potato did not move.

What worked, in a single run

7,448 photographs taken on real farms — 6,152 of maize and tomato from Ghana, 1,296 of potato taken on smartphones in Central Java. Training went from 54% field imagery to 78%.

Tomato 44→88%, potato 50→86%, maize 65→82%. The bottleneck was never the training method. It was photographs that look like what a farmer takes.

When the app declines to answer

The app withholds a diagnosis below a confidence threshold and asks for a clearer photograph. That threshold belongs to the model and was re-measured — a better-calibrated model pushes more answers above any fixed cut, so an inherited number quietly stops doing its job.

These are not Indian field conditions. The new photographs are from Ghana and Indonesia. They are real farm photography, which is what mattered, but they are not photographs of Indian farms taken on the phones our users carry. The true figure here remains unmeasured.

Two potato classes carry known label noise. The source's “fungi” folder is early blight together with at least one other fungal disease. It was used deliberately: the alternative added late-blight examples and no early-blight ones, which would have pushed the model further towards an answer it already over-gives.

Advice is bounded. Every answer now carries a note to confirm with the local Krishi Vigyan Kendra before buying any chemical, and for rice bacterial blight and tungro the app deliberately recommends no spray — none works, and selling a farmer one costs money for nothing.
